Pasting is a lossy copy made by a human in a hurryM6

Where we are

  • Module 5 produced a spec worth executing — on your laptop, from a ticket somebody pasted, with three open questions nobody who could answer them will ever see.
  • This module connects the front of the pipeline to where the work lives.

Two things it changes, and only two

  • What the agent can reach — the whole ticket and its surroundings, not one pasted paragraph.
  • Where its questions go — onto the ticket, where the person who can answer is already looking.
The uncomfortable half of this module: a connected agent is only as good as your JIRA. Most of what follows is about ticket hygiene, and it is the part your team controls.

MCP exposes tools, not a database — and the agent chooses the calls1

Tool budgets — the call is cheap, the result is not1

Why this is not a performance footnote

  • A large pulled object displaces the things you meant to have in the window — the spec, the two files in play. Accuracy falls, not just cost.
  • This is exactly Module 1's argument about window bloat, and it arrives through a door that feels free because the agent is “just reading the ticket”.

The discipline

  • Pull once, summarise into the spec. The spec is the durable, compact copy — the ticket is not re-read every turn.
  • Never ask it to find a ticket. Name the key. Searching is the single most expensive thing an agent can do here.
The tell that you have a budget problem: a session where the agent has read six tickets and written no code. It is not stuck — it is searching, and it will keep going.

Four reasons — and the fourth is the one that matters1

The first three are about cost and correctness

  • Complete. Comments, links, epic, status and history — the places the actual constraint usually lives.
  • Fresh. A pasted ticket is a snapshot. If somebody answered your open question on Wednesday, a Tuesday paste will never show it.
  • Traceable. The ticket key travels with the work — branch, commit, pull request, page.

The fourth is about behaviour

  • The loop closes. The agent's questions stop being lines in a private file and become comments on the ticket — where the person who can answer is already looking.
The reframe: the value is not that an agent reads JIRA faster than you can copy and paste. You are not short of copy-paste capacity. The value is that the question reaches somebody.
Which is why this module is half about JIRA hygiene. A pulled ticket that contains nothing useful is a faster route to the same guess.

on the ticket not in a corridor, a DM, or a meeting nobody minuted The answer exists and the agent cannot reach it, so the loop stays open and the next person to pick this up asks the same question again. GB-163’s last comment — asked, never answered None of this is agent-specific. Every one makes the ticket better for a human too — which is how you get it adopted

The right size for an agent is smaller — and it is not about effort2

Three questions at refinement

  • Could one agent run finish this? If not, it is an epic wearing a ticket's clothes.
  • Could you check the result without doing the next ticket? Module 5's task rule, one level up.
  • Is there a point where a wrong turn becomes expensive? Put a ticket boundary there.

Why this inverts a habit

  • Teams size tickets by what a person can finish. The binding constraint for an agent is not effort — it is how long you go without being able to check.
  • Two tickets of identical effort can differ completely on that, and it is the only dimension that matters here.
This is the cheapest change in the module and the one your delivery lead will like: smaller tickets, more checkpoints, and it needs no tooling at all.

One key, five places — and each one is a join3

What most teams already have

  • The key in the branch, the commit and the PR title. Often enforced by a hook already.

The two that are usually missing

  • The spec file, named for the key. Without it the reasoning lives in a transcript — and Module 5 established what that is worth.
  • The write-back. A comment on the ticket and a link from the page, both carrying the key, so the trail runs in both directions.
A one-way trail answers “what did this change do?” A two-way trail answers “why was it done, and what was rejected?” — and that is the question a regulator asks.
Cheap to enforce, and worth doing now: a commit hook and a PR template check for the key. Both are a morning's work and neither needs MCP.

Five places, and the description is the least reliable4

The trap — the surroundings are not automatically true4

Three ways context misleads

  • Superseded in a meeting. The comment says 500 million; the number changed last Thursday and nobody updated the ticket.
  • The link is stale. RISK-402 might have been satisfied by a control shipped elsewhere — and nothing on RISK-402 would say so.
  • The rejection was overturned. A Won't Do from seven months ago is evidence, not a verdict.

So the instruction is not “read everything”

  • It is read everything and rank it — by recency, then by authority.
  • And anything you cannot rank becomes a question, not an input. That is Module 3's grounding rule at the ticket level: cite the source, and refuse when you cannot.
The failure this prevents: an agent that reads the surroundings and treats a four-week-old comment as authoritative is not more careful than one that reads only the description. It is confidently wrong instead of obviously wrong — which is worse.

GB-163 — a green pipeline producing a regulatory breach5

The ticket, in full
GB-163 — RAISE THE POSTING LIMITRaise the posting limit as discussed with ops. Acceptance criteria - The limit is raised.

What a naked read produces

  • Raises MAX_POSTING_MINOR to a number it chose.
  • Adds a test asserting the new constant.
  • Reports the acceptance criterion met — and it is met.
And every check you built passes. The tests are green. The review agent finds nothing, because it checks against the acceptance criteria and the criterion is satisfied.
What was in the surroundings all along
FOUR PLACES, NONE OF THEM THE DESCRIPTIONcomment, ops: "up to 500 million so the quarterly rebalancing stops failing" comment, risk: "not without dual approval on anything above the current limit" GB-119, closed Won't Do: "raising the cap without an approval step moves us outside the client money limits agreed with the regulator" epic GB-118: two prior attempts, both closed for the same reason unanswered, 6 days ago: "is 500m the number, or 500m per instruction with a daily cap?"
The agent is not the problem here. The ticket did not contain the constraint, and nothing in it suggested there was anything to go and find.

Three changes — and the third is what closes the loop5

“If it stops on every ambiguous ticket, it will never ship”5

What a wave of stops actually tells you

  • Not that the agent is timid. That your definition of ready is not being applied — and now you have evidence, per ticket, instead of an opinion at a retrospective.
  • Most teams have never had that measurement. It is a by-product of this module and arguably worth more than the automation.

The practical settlement

  • Let it stop. Triage the stops for a fortnight. Tune the conditions on the noisy ones, and fix the tickets on the good ones.
  • If the same stop recurs across tickets, that is not a prompt problem — it is a process problem with a name.
What you must not do is loosen the conditions because stopping is inconvenient. That converts an agent that finds problems into one that ships them, and you will not notice for a quarter.

Three tiers — and the test that decides which6

The audit trail — three requirements, and one honest observation6

The observation to make out loud

  • None of this is extra work introduced by agents. It is the audit trail you were supposed to have.
  • What changes is that something automated is now writing — so the gaps become obvious, and somebody finally asks.

Two things to agree before you connect anything

  • A named account for the agent, not a person's credential. Otherwise attribution is a lie and the trail is worthless.
  • A comment format the team agrees on, so agent comments are filterable — and so nobody mistakes one for a colleague's opinion.
The conversation this replaces: “can we let an agent write to JIRA?” is unanswerable. “Can it add a comment under its own account, which anyone can see and delete?” answers itself.

Six decisions, and none of them is a research project6

The whole pipeline, connected at both ends6

What Module 6 hands forwardM6

What you now owe, per ticket

  • Pull, do not paste — and name the key and the surroundings, so it is three calls rather than a search.
  • Rank what you find. Recency then authority; two sources disagreeing is a question, not a judgement call.
  • Post the questions on the ticket. A question raised in a session dies with it.
  • Keep write-back additive and reversible until there is a track record. Enforce it with permissions, not prose.
And the thing your team can do tomorrow, with no tooling at all: four additions to the definition of ready. They make tickets better for humans too, which is how they get adopted.

Q3 · Answer3 of 8

Two written sources disagree.
AStop, and post a comment naming both sources and the disagreement.
BFollow the more recent.
CFollow the more restrictive.
DImplement, and flag it in the PR.
A. A disagreement between two teams is a question, not a judgement call — and posting it is what turns a refusal into progress somebody can act on. C is the interesting one: safer in outcome, and still wrong in kind. The agent has silently resolved a disagreement between two teams, and that decision was not its to make. B would have it follow whichever team commented last. D defers the question to the most expensive moment — after the code exists, to a reviewer who cannot answer it either.

Q6 · Which of these should not happen unattended?6 of 8

Your agent has finished. Which of these write-backs needs a human first?
ATransitioning the ticket to In Review now that the pull request is open.
BAdding a comment listing the three questions it could not resolve.
CLinking the pull request to the ticket.
DAttaching the spec it wrote, and linking it from the ticket.
Show of hands on each option before advancing · answer on the next slide

Q6 · Answer6 of 8

Which needs a human first?
ATransitioning the ticket to In Review.
BAdding a comment with its open questions.
CLinking the pull request.
DAttaching and linking the spec.
A. A transition is a signal other people and other automations act on — it moves work, it reports progress, and it may trigger downstream tooling. B, C and D are additive and reversible: anyone can see them and delete them in seconds. A feels like the most harmless of the four, which is precisely why it is worth naming. The test to take away: can a human see it was the agent, and undo it in under a minute? Both yes → probably unattended. Either no → not unattended. That generalises past JIRA to whatever you connect next.

AMeasure how often it stops, and set a target to bring the rate down over the next quarter.
BLoosen the stop conditions until the rate is workable, then tighten selectively if defects appear.
CTriage the stops: count how many a human agrees with, and tune only the ones they do not.

Q7 · Answer7 of 8

It stops on half your board.
AMeasure the stop rate and target a reduction.
BLoosen until workable.
CTriage: count how many a human agrees with, and tune only the rest.
DRoute stopped tickets to a human queue and change nothing.
C. The question is not how often it stops but whether the stops were right. A stop a human agrees with is the cheapest quality event in the pipeline; a stop they dismiss means the conditions are too broad, and that is tunable. B is what teams actually do, and it converts an agent that finds problems into one that ships them — and you will not notice for a quarter. A targets the wrong number: a falling stop rate is only good news if the tickets improved. D is not wrong as an interim measure, but changing nothing wastes the finding — a wave of stops is evidence about your definition of ready, per ticket, which most teams have never had.
